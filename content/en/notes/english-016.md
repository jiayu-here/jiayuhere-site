## 13.1 Basic Concepts of Relative Clauses
### 13.1.1 Modifiers
**Relative Clauses and Complex Sentences**
- A relative clause is a subordinate clause within a complex sentence;

**What is a modifier?**
- A modifier adds information about a noun;
- 1. A word or phrase is often enough when little information is needed;
- 2. When more information is needed, a relative clause can be placed after the noun to identify or describe it;

### 13.1.1 Relative Clause
**Basic Concepts of Relative Clauses**
- Core: A relative clause follows and modifies an antecedent noun;
- Structure:
	- In English, `which` introduces a clause that modifies the preceding noun `discipline`;
	- Example: *Law is a discipline that encourages responsible judgment.*;
	- ![Pasted image 20240316010531](/assets/notes/8cd7fbef6cff-Pasted-image-20240316010531.png)

**Antecedents and Relative Pronouns/Adverbs**
- Give an example
	- *Law is <u>a discipline</u> <u>which</u> encourages responsible judgment.*
		- Before splitting:
		- 1. Law is a discipline.
		- 2. A discipline encourages responsible judgment.
	- `a discipline`: the antecedent being modified;
	- `which`: a relative pronoun that refers to `a discipline` and functions as the subject of the relative clause;
- Functions of relative words
	- 1. Introduce the relative clause;
	- 2. Perform a grammatical role in the relative clause while referring back to the antecedent;


## 13.2 Use of Relative Clauses
### 13.2.1 Writing Relative Clauses
**Structure of Relative Clauses**
- Position: placed after the antecedent (n.).
- Writing Method:
	- 1. Start from two complete clauses that contain the same noun;
	- 2. Turn the repeated noun in the second clause into an appropriate relative pronoun or adverb, then place the relative clause after its antecedent;
- Illustration:

### 13.2.2 Antecedents and Relative Words
**Choosing Antecedents and Relative Words**
- Antecedent: a thing
	- Relative word: which/that
- Antecedent: a person
	- Relative words: who/whom/that
	- Note: `who` may be a subject or object; formal English uses `whom` only as an object;
- Antecedent: a person’s or thing’s (indicates all relationships between people or things, "someone's/something's")
	- Relative word: whose
- Antecedent: time
	- Relative word: when
- Antecedent: place
	- Relative word: where
	- Note: `where` can refer to a literal place or to an abstract situation, field, or context;

**Examples**
- 1. The antecedent is things or objects, and the relative word is which/that;
	- *Teachers need to be aware of the emotional, intellectual, and physical changes ___ young adults experience.*
		- Use `that` or `which`: the clause modifies `changes`, which functions as the object of `experience`;
	- *Furthermore, the legal system and the events <u>which</u> occur within it are primary subjects for journalists.*
		- The antecedent `events` denotes things, so either `which` or `that` is possible in a restrictive clause;
- 2. The antecedent is 'person', and the relative word is chosen as who/whom/that;
	- *In France, shareholders <u>who/that</u> ==hold onto a company investment for at least two years== can sometimes earn more voting rights in a company.*
		- In France, shareholders must have held shares in the company for at least two years to be eligible for voting rights;
		- `shareholders` refers to people and is the subject of `hold`, so use `who` or `that`;
- 3. The antecedent is people or things, indicating all relationships between people or things; the relational word is 'whose';
	- *It also pledged not to deploy AI <u>whose</u> use would violate international laws or human rights.*
		- `whose use` means “the use of that AI”;
		- `whose` can express possession or association for both people and things;
	- *This, for those as yet unaware of such a disadvantage, refers to discrimination against those <u>whose</u> surnames begin with a letter in the lower half of the alphabet.*
- 4. The antecedent is time, and the relative word is chosen when;
	- *It is difficult to the point of impossibility for the average reader under the age of forty to imagine a time ___ high-quality arts criticism could be found in most big-city newspapers.*
		- Fill in when;
- 5. The antecedent is 'location', and the relative word is 'where';
	- *Today, we live in a world <u>where</u> GPS systems, digital maps, and other navigation apps are all available on our smartphones.*
- 6. When the antecedent is `reason`, the relative adverb may be `why`;
	- *The other reason <u>why</u> costs are so high is the restrictive guild-like ownership structure of the business.*

### 13.2.3 Notes on Relative Words
1. Every relative pronoun has a grammatical role in its clause; `that` may refer to either people or things;
2. With things, restrictive relative clauses may use `which` or `that`. A non-restrictive clause after a comma uses `which`, not `that`;
3. `whom` refers to a person and functions only as an object; `who` may function as a subject or object, while `that` may function as either in a restrictive clause;
4. When a relative word is used as an object in a relative clause (after a transitive verb or after a preposition), it can be omitted;
5. Supplement: Special relational word whereby = by which

## 13.3 Restrictive and Non-Restrictive Relative Clauses
### 13.3.1 Basic Concepts
**Examples**
- Restrictive relative clause: *He will call his friend who is working in London.*
- Non-restrictive relative clause (absolutely explicit):*He will call his mother, who is working in London.*
- Non-restrictive relative clause (the antecedent is already identified): *This trend, which we believe is still in its infancy, effectively began with retailers and travel providers such as airlines and hotels and will no doubt go further.*

### 13.3.2 Supplement 1: Restrictive Relative Clauses and Appositive Clauses
**Differences Between Restrictive and Appositive Clauses**
- Appositive clause
	- Purpose: $\text{ Explanation }:\,n.$
	- Location: ` abstract n. `
	- Role of `that`: in an appositive/content clause, `that` is only a subordinator and has no grammatical role inside the clause;
- A restrictive relative clause
	- Purpose: identify or describe a noun;
	- Position: after the antecedent noun, whether concrete or abstract;
	- Role of `that`: `that` refers to the antecedent and functions as the subject or object within the relative clause;

**Examples**
- *One possible response is for classical performers to program attractive new ==music== <u>that</u> is not yet available on record.*
	- music: antecedent;
	- that: relative word;
	- 'music' is not an abstract noun, so it is followed by a relative clause rather than an appositive clause;
	- And 'that' serves as the component, so it can also be shown that this is a relative clause;
- *The ==idea== <u>that</u> some groups of people may be more intelligent than others is one of ==those hypotheses== <u>that</u> dare not speak its name.*
	- This sentence has two 'that' clauses, both followed by the noun -> and then the noun, so it is either an appositive clause or a relative clause;
	- The first `that`-clause explains the content of *the idea*, so it is an appositive/content clause;
	- The second 'that' serves as a component, expressing... such an assumption, so it is a relative clause;
- *... We unconsciously imitate the behavior (<u>that</u>) **we see every day**.*
	- The word 'that' here is omitted;
	- Conclusion: A `noun + that-clause` sequence may be either a relative clause or an appositive/content clause; decide by checking whether `that` has a grammatical role inside the clause;

### 13.3.3 Supplement 2: Relative Clauses Referring to an Entire Clause
**Special non-restrictive relative clauses that comment on the whole preceding clause**
- Usage:
	- 1. When adding to the whole sentence, only $\text{ Comma }+which$ or $\text{ Comma }+as$ can be used
	- 2. `which` normally follows the clause it refers to; `as` may appear before or after the main clause in patterns such as `as is known`;

**Examples**
- *Most fisheries are well below that, <u>which</u> is a bad way to do business.*
	- which modifies the entire previous matter, thus modifies the whole sentence;
- *A few generative rules are then sufficient to unfold the entire fundamental structure of a language, <u>which</u> is why children can learn it so quickly.*
	- which modifies the entire previous matter;
	- Here, `which is why` is a fixed relationship and cannot simply be replaced with `as`;
- *Nevertheless, <u>as</u> any biographer knows, a person's early life and its conditions are often the greatest gift to an individual.*
	- As indicates the whole thing;

### 13.3.4 Supplement 3: Preposition Fronting in Relative Clauses
**Formal pattern**: $\text{preposition}+\text{whom/which}$
- A preposition may be stranded at the end of the relative clause or fronted before the relative pronoun;
- After a fronted preposition, use `which` for things and `whom` for people—not `that` or `who`;

**Examples**
- *Grade forgiveness allows students to retake a course <u>which</u> ==they received a low grade== <u>in</u>...*
	- With preposition stranding, `in` remains at the end of the clause;
	- With preposition fronting: *Grade forgiveness allows students to retake a course <u>in which</u> they received a low grade...*
- *... Commercial genetic testing is only as good as the reference collections <u>which</u> ==a sample is compared== <u>to</u>.*
	- `as ... as` expresses a comparison of equality;
	- Front `to`: *Commercial genetic testing is only as good as the reference collections <u>to which</u> a sample is compared.*
- *Television is one of the means <u>by which</u> these feelings are created and conveyed...*
	- by which: through

### 13.3.5 Key Points
1. Overview: a relative clause follows and modifies an antecedent noun;
2. Structure: relative pronoun/adverb + clause; choose the relative word according to the antecedent and its role inside the clause;
3. Classification of relative clauses: restrictive relative clauses and non-restrictive relative clauses;
4. Supplementation of relative clauses
	(1) The difference between restrictive relative clauses and appositive/content clauses
	(2) Special non-restrictive relative clauses (supplementary explanation of the entire sentence)
	(3) Relative clauses with a fronted preposition
