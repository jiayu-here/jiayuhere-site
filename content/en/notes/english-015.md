## 12.1 Complex Sentences
**Introduction**
- When two sentences are equally important, you can use a compound sentence;
- When one proposition is subordinate to another, use a complex sentence containing a main clause and one or more subordinate clauses;
	- Primary proposition: main clause
	- Subordinate proposition: subordinate clause
- The main focus is how subordinate clauses are formed and used.

### 12.1.1 Composition of Complex Sentences
**Composition Analysis**
- Main clause: *America's new plan to buy up toxic assets will not work.*
- Clause 1: *<u>unless</u> banks mark assets to levels*
- Clause 2: *<u>which</u> buyers find attractive.*
- The main clause is a regular sentence, with a conjunction added before the clause;

### 12.1.2 Classification of clauses
- Section 1: Noun clauses
- Section 2: Relative clauses
- Section 3: Adverbial clauses

## 12.2 What is a nominal clause?
**Concept**
- A clause used as a noun in a sentence is called a noun clause;
- Four types of noun clauses
	- 1. Object Clauses (Most Tested)
	- 2. Predicative Clauses (Slightly less tested)
	- 3. Subject Clauses (Also Important)
	- 4. Appositive Clauses (Also Important)
- Similarities: The writing method is basically the same, just the position differs -> Once you master one method, the other three methods are similar;

## 12.3 Usage of Object Clauses
### 12.3.1 Basics of Object Clauses
**What is an object clause**
- Core concept: a subordinate clause functions as the object of a verb, preposition, or adjective in the main clause;
- Illustration:
	- Judge Kennedy wrote that the previous resolution was flawed;
	- ![Pasted image 20240313195420](/assets/notes/5821b639604c-Pasted-image-20240313195420.png)

### 12.3.2 Writing Object Clauses
**How to Write Object Clauses**
- Core:
	- 1. An embedded object clause normally uses declarative word order;
	- 2. Even when the main clause is a question, the embedded clause does not use question inversion;
	- 3. In short: **use declarative word order inside an object clause**;
- 1. Turn a statement into a `that`-clause
	- Add `that` before the statement and place the clause after the verb or expression that selects it;
	- *Dr. Worm acknowledges （that）<u>these figures are conservative</u>.*
		- that: Although omitted, it is not recommended to omit it;
	- *We believe （that）<u>consumers should have more control</u>.*
- 2. Turn a wh-question into an embedded clause
	- Keep the wh-word, but restore declarative subject–finite verb order;
	- *We suddenly can't remember <u>where we put the keys just a moment ago</u>.*
- 3. Turn a yes–no question into an embedded clause
	- Add `if` or `whether` before the clause; `whether` is often preferable in formal writing;
	- Yes–no question: Are other clients going to abandon me, too?
		- Answers generally only include yes or no;
	- After revision: *I don't know <u>if other clients are going to abandon me, too</u>....*

### 12.3.3 Usage and Position of Object Clauses
**Summary: Usage of Object Clauses**
- Common principles
	- 1. Embedded clauses normally use declarative word order;
	- 2. A subordinator or interrogative word introduces the clause;
- Method
	- 1. Put the embedded proposition in declarative word order;
	- 2. Choose an appropriate introductory word;
	- 3. Choose it according to the missing meaning or grammatical role:
		- 1. Time: `when`
		- 2. Place: `where`
		- 3. Person: `who`
		- 4. Complete statement with no missing element: `that`
- Example:
	- I know what you bought at the store yesterday;
		- *I know <u>what</u> you bought at the store yesterday.*

**Summary: Position of Object Clauses**
- Core concept: Object clauses occur in three common grammatical environments;
- 1. Verb object:
	- Verb and simple object: transitive verb + clause;
	- Double-object pattern: transitive verb + indirect object (person) + object clause;
- 2. Object of a preposition;
- 3. Adjective + object clause (just understand);

**Verb + Single Object Clause**
- *... Scientists believe <u>that intelligence can expand and fluctuate according to mental effort</u>.*
	- The `that`-clause answers “believe what?” and functions as the direct object of `believe`;
- *... Users could tell advertisers that <u>they did not want to be followed</u>*
- Supplement: non-finite verb + object
	- *.. She wanted to know <u>if vaccines come from animal research</u>.*
- Supplement: Multiple object clauses
	- *To filter out <u>what</u> is unique from <u>what</u> is shared might enable us to understand <u>how</u> ==complex cultural behavior arose== **and** <u>what</u> ==guides it in evolutionary or cognitive terms==.*
	- `what is unique`: an object clause after `filter out`;
	- from what: preposition-object clause;
* Supplement: A long object clause may be extraposed, with dummy `it` in the usual object position;
	* *That kind of activity makes <u>it</u> less likely <u>that the court's decisions will be accepted as impartial judgments</u>*
	* `it` is the dummy object; the `that`-clause is the extraposed semantic object;
	* Why is the object clause placed later:
		* 1. In subject-verb-object-object complement, the object is followed by an object complement, so keeping a long object clause in its usual position would make the sentence difficult to process;
		* 2. The object is too long;

## 12.4 Predicative Clauses
### 12.4.1 What is a predicative clause?
**What is a predicative clause**
- Core concept: a subordinate clause functions as the subject complement after a linking verb;
- Illustration:
	- ![Pasted image 20240314122836](/assets/notes/5c60524dba4d-Pasted-image-20240314122836.png)

### 12.4.2 Writing predicative clauses
**How to write predicative clauses**: The method is the same as for object clauses
- Illustration:
	- ![Pasted image 20240314123004](/assets/notes/471f123e625f-Pasted-image-20240314123004.png)
- Note: 'That' in object clauses can be omitted, but 'that' in predicative clauses cannot be omitted;

**Examples**
- *Part of the issue is <u>that</u> airports have only so much room for screening lanes.*
	- Part of the problem is that airports have limited space;
- *This is <u>where</u> developing new habits comes in.*
- *This is <u>why</u> repeated attempts at reform legislation have failed in recent years.*

### 12.4.3 Position of predicative clauses
**Composition**
- Subject + linking verb + subject complement
- Common linking verbs:
	- 1. Forms of `be`
	- 2. Sensory linking verbs such as `look`, `sound`, and `seem`
	- 3. Verbs such as `remain` and `appear`
- A clause that follows a linking verb and functions as the subject complement is a predicative clause;
	- The most common is following a form of “be” used as the main linking verb;

**Examples**
- *yet the fact ==remains== <u>that</u> the merger movement must be watched*
	- `remains`: linking verb;
- *Human nature ==being== <u>what it is</u>, most people stick with default settings.*
- *The explanation for insensitivity to smell seems ==to be== <u>that</u> <span style="background:#d3f8b6">the brain finds it inefficient to keep all smell receptors working all the time but can create new receptors if necessary</span>.*
	- After the verb 'be', it is followed by a long predicative clause;

## 12.5 Subject Clauses
**This is an important exam point**
### 12.5.1 What is a subject clause?
**What is a subject clause**
- Core concept: a subordinate clause functions as the subject of the main clause;
- Illustration:
	- ![Pasted image 20240314123935](/assets/notes/7a847e6277b8-Pasted-image-20240314123935.png)

### 12.5.2 Writing Subject Clauses
**How to write subject clauses**: Use the same internal word order as other noun clauses
- 1. In the subject clause, 'that' must be kept;
- 2. Use `whether`, not `if`, for an initial subject clause;
- Composition:
	- ![Pasted image 20240314124135](/assets/notes/69438f75e7b6-Pasted-image-20240314124135.png)

**Examples**
- *<u>That</u> <span style="background:#d3f8b6">the seas are being overfished</span> has been known for years.*
- *And <u>whether</u> the community's work contributes much to an overall accumulation of knowledge is doubtful.*
	- The opening `And` links this sentence to the previous one; it is not part of the subject clause;
	- The entire `whether`-clause is the subject of `is doubtful`;

### 12.5.3 Position of the subject clause
**Composition**
- Core concept: The same subject clause can be placed at the beginning or end; their meanings are the same;
- First type: at the beginning of a sentence
	- Note: At the beginning of a sentence = placed before the finite verb, not necessarily at the very beginning (for example, the very front may be an and);
- Second: Place at the end of the sentence
	- Note: Use dummy subject `it`, then place the extraposed subject clause later;
- Supplement: Initial and extraposed subject clauses can express the same proposition. Extraposition is often more natural when the clause is long; it is not inherently “more advanced”;

**Examples**
- *Just <u>how</u> people think is still far too complex to be understood...*
	- The subject clause is at the beginning of the sentence;
	- how: How people think about this is already far too complicated;
	- Natural reformulation: *<font color="#92d050">It</font> is still far too complex to understand just <u>how</u> people think.*
- *<u>What</u> is needed is a technology of behavior...*
	- The subject clause is at the beginning of the sentence;
	- This initial `what`-clause is already natural; `*It is a technology of behavior what is needed` is not a grammatical extraposition;
- *It did not matter <u>what</u> was done in the experiment...*
	- The subject clause is at the end of the sentence (this is the most commonly used in graduate entrance exams);
	- Forward: *What was done in the experiment did not matter...*
- *<u>It</u> is becoming less clear, however, <u>that</u> <span style="background:#d3f8b6">such a theory would be a simplification...</span>*

## 12.6 Appositive Clauses
### 12.6.1 What is an appositive clause?
**What is an appositive clause**
- Core Concepts:
	- 1. An appositive/content clause follows a noun within the main clause;
	- 2. It states the content of an abstract noun such as `fact`, `idea`, `belief`, or `evidence`;
- Note: Not all nouns require explanation. Nouns that require explanation: **abstract nouns (facts, ideas)**;
- Illustration:
	- `that` introduces a clause giving the content of the preceding abstract noun;
	- ![Pasted image 20240314153819](/assets/notes/2fd77949847f-Pasted-image-20240314153819.png)

### 12.6.2 Writing Appositive Clauses
**How to write appositive clauses**: Use the same internal word order as other noun clauses
- Place the clause after the abstract noun it explains;
	- The most common pattern is `abstract noun + that + declarative word order`;
- Illustration:
	- ![Pasted image 20240314154104](/assets/notes/5d6ccd40c1fc-Pasted-image-20240314154104.png)

**Examples**
- *Evidence <u>that</u> <span style="background:#d3f8b6">the Lovelife program produces lasting changes</span> is limited and mixed.*
	- *Evidence is limited and mixed.*
	- Used to explain evidence: *the Lovelife program produces lasting changes.*
- *Since plants don't have nerve systems, the chances <u>that</u> <span style="background:#d3f8b6">they have consciousness are effectively zero</span>.*
	- The `that`-clause gives the content of `the chances`;
	- Main structure: `the chances ... are effectively zero`;
- *For my part, I have no idea <u>whether</u> Gilbert is a great conductor or even a good one.*
	- The `whether`-clause gives the content of `idea`;

### 12.6.3 Position of appositive clauses
**Position of appositive clauses**
- Core: placed after the abstract noun being explained;
- Abstract nouns: **idea, opinion, view, fact, evidence, question, doubt, reason, demand, theory, belief, possibility, chance, hope, contention, guarantee...**

**Examples**
- *In other words, they all share a view <u>that</u> <span style="background:#d3f8b6">the international tax system has failed to keep up with the current economy</span>.*
	- Explain abstract nouns like view;
- *But the idea <u>that</u> <span style="background:#d3f8b6">the journalist must understand the law more profoundly than an ordinary citizen</span> rests on an understanding of the established conventions and special responsibilities of the news media.*

## 12.7 Summary
### 12.7.1 Key Points
1. Nominal clauses are divided into four categories: object clauses, predicative clauses, subject clauses, appositive clauses;
2. Noun clauses generally use an introductory word plus declarative word order;
3. Choose the introductory word according to meaning and grammatical role. Use `that` for a complete statement; `that` may often be omitted in an object clause;
4. The four types are distinguished by their grammatical positions and functions;

**Position of Nominal Clauses**
- Object clause
	- Verb object
		- Verb and simple object: transitive verb + clause
		- Double-object pattern: transitive verb + indirect object (person) + object clause
	- Object of a preposition
	- Adjective + object clause
- Predicative clause:
	- After a linking verb (most commonly a form of “be”)
- Subject clause
	- Sentence beginning
	- Extraposed after dummy subject `it`
- Appositive clause
	- After an abstract noun

### 12.7.2 Why Study Nominal Clauses
**When expressing a concept, it is often necessary to use nominal clauses**
- When a sentence contains more information and a phrase cannot express meaning, clauses need to be used to convey information;
- Many essay templates use noun clauses;
