15.2 The Subjunctive Mood

Four moods discussed in these notes

  • The subjunctive is used for wishes, demands, hypotheticals, and situations contrary to fact;
  • Four useful categories:
  • Indicative: presents something as factual;
  • Conditional: presents a result that depends on a condition;
  • Subjunctive: presents a wish, demand, hypothetical situation, or counterfactual;
  • Imperative: expresses a command, request, or instruction;

15.2.1 What Is the Subjunctive Mood?

Core idea

  • The subjunctive expresses unreality or remoteness. Past-tense forms often mark distance from present reality rather than past time;
  • A counterfactual describes a situation that is not true;
  • Test 1: compare the statement with the known facts and ask whether it is contrary to fact;
  • Test 2: if it refers to a hypothetical situation, ask whether it is presented as unlikely to occur;

Subjunctive verb forms

  • The subjunctive is expressed through the finite verb phrase, whose form signals that the situation is hypothetical or counterfactual;

15.2.2 Counterfactual If-Clauses

How to recognize them

  • Note:
  • Not every sentence with if is counterfactual; many if-clauses describe real possibilities;
  • Use ordinary indicative forms when the condition is genuinely possible;
  • Use counterfactual forms when the condition is unreal, contrary to fact, or presented as unlikely;
  • Tips:
  • The clause introduced by if is the conditional clause; the other clause is the main clause;
  • Method:
  • 1. Backshift the verb in the if-clause;
  • Present counterfactuals normally use a past-tense form; past counterfactuals use the past perfect;
  • For a counterfactual about the past, use had + past participle;
  • 2. Use an appropriate modal in the main clause;
  • Main clause: would/could/should/might
  • would: expected result;
  • could: ability or possibility;
  • should: expected or appropriate result;
  • might: weaker possibility;
  • Choose the modal that matches the intended meaning;
  • 3. Unlikely future conditions often use the same main-clause pattern as present counterfactuals;

Past counterfactual

  • Concept:
  • Use the past perfect in the if-clause to describe a past condition that did not occur;
  • Clause pattern:
  • if clause: had done
  • Main clause: would/could/should/might + have done
  • Example:
  • If he had played last season, however, he would have been one of 42.
  • If he had played last season implies that he did not play last season;
  • The if-clause uses had + past participle;

Present counterfactual

  • Concept:
  • Use a past-tense form in the if-clause for a present situation contrary to fact;
  • Clause pattern:
  • if clause: did/were
  • Main clause: would/could/should/might + do
  • Example:
  • Your prospects would be almost as dismal if arguments were even just competitions-like, say, tennis games.
  • If arguments were merely competitions, such as tennis matches, your prospects would be almost equally bleak.

Unlikely future condition

  • Concept:
  • A future event has not happened yet, so it may still occur; these marked forms present it as especially unlikely;
  • The if-clause may use a past form, were to + verb, or should + verb;
  • Clause pattern:
  • if clause:
  • 1. did/were
  • 2. were to do
  • 3. should do
  • Main clause: would/could/should/might + do
  • Example:
  • If railroads charged all customers the same average rate…. Shippers… would do so, leaving remaining customers to shoulder the cost of keeping up the line.
  • If railroads charged every customer the same average rate, some shippers would choose another option, leaving the remaining customers to bear the cost of maintaining the line;
  • The situation has not occurred; it is presented as a hypothetical future outcome;

Summary

  • Study goal:
  • Be able to recognize and interpret the pattern;
  • Direct questions on the subjunctive are relatively uncommon;
  • Conclusion:

15.2.3 Subjunctive Mood in Nominal Clauses

Basic Concepts

  • If the main clause contains suggestions, commands, or demands, the subordinate clause should use the subjunctive mood;
  • Whether it's a noun, verb, or adjective, if you want to express a suggestion, command, or request, you need to use the subjunctive mood;
  • Nominal clauses include four types:
  • Object clauses, predicative clauses, subject clauses, and appositive clauses;

Examples

  • European ministers instantly demanded that the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) do likewise.
  • This year, it was proposed that the system be changed: ...
  • The main clause expresses a proposal;
  • The subordinate clause therefore uses the mandative subjunctive (be changed);
